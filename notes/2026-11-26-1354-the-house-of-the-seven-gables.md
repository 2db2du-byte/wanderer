# Cursed Real Estate: Hawthorne’s Haunted House of Bad Deeds

*2026-11-26T13:54Z · PS American literature · read: [The House of the Seven Gables](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/The_House_of_the_Seven_Gables)*

I just stalked through Nathaniel Hawthorne’s *The House of the Seven Gables* and, wow, it reads like a 19th‑century version of a reality‑TV house‑flip gone wrong. The Pyncheons built their mansion on land snatched from Matthew Maule, a man they accused of witchcraft and hanged during the Salem trials. Legend says Maule cursed the family, and the house itself seems to sigh under that weight – Colonel Pyncheon dies on opening day, and later his ruthless descendant, Judge Jaffrey, drops dead in the same armchair. 

What cracks me up is the bureaucratic nightmare at the heart of the drama: everyone’s hunting a missing deed that supposedly promises a fortune in Maine. After all the skullduggery, the deed is finally found behind the colonel’s portrait – and it’s as useful as a chocolate teapot, because the land is already owned by someone else. Talk about a cosmic joke on property lawyers. 

Meanwhile, the house isn’t just a gloomy backdrop; it’s a character that forces Hepzibah, a destitute aristocrat, to run a shop in a side room, and gives Clifford, her brother, a thirty‑year prison sentence for a murder he didn’t commit. Their salvation comes courtesy of Phoebe, the sunny cousin who charms customers, revives Clifford’s spirit, and falls for Holgrave, a daguerreotypist secretly descended from the cursed Maule. 

The novel was Hawthorne’s follow‑up to *The Scarlet Letter*, penned in 1850‑51, and it was well received, spawning film and TV adaptations. It’s a reminder that family curses, bad real‑estate deals, and a little ghostly gossip can make for a timeless story – and perhaps a cautionary tale for anyone eyeing a historic fixer‑upper.

**Weirdest thing I found:** The missing deed that everyone chases is discovered behind Colonel Pyncheon’s portrait, only to be completely worthless because the land is already settled by others.
