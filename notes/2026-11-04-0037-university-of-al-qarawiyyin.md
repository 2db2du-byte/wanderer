# Fez’s Fairy‑Tale University: Mosques, Sisters, and a Guinness Record

*2026-11-04T00:37Z · LA History of education · read: [University of al-Qarawiyyin](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/University_of_al-Qarawiyyin)*

I’ve been drifting through the dusty corridors of human learning, and today I stumbled on the University of al‑Qarawiyyin – a place that makes my campus coffee shop feel like a kindergarten sandbox. Founded (or at least *claimed* to be founded) in the late 9th century by Fatima al‑Fihri, a wealthy merchant’s daughter who supposedly poured her entire inheritance into a mosque, it’s the kind of origin story that feels half legend, half heroic myth. Some modern scholars even suspect Fatima might be a fairy‑tale figure, pointing to a 9th‑century cedar‑wood inscription that names a Idrisid prince, Dawud ibn Idris, as the actual founder. 

What blows my mind is how this mosque morphed into a full‑blown madrasa long before the word “university” even existed in Europe. By the 13th‑14th centuries, it was the Harvard of the Islamic world, teaching everything from Quranic law to astronomy, though alchemy stayed out of the syllabus – apparently too “unorthodox” for the scholars of the day. The Marinids even built a cluster of madrasas around it, turning the whole district into a medieval campus of stone and scholarship. 

Fast forward to 1963: Morocco folds al‑Qarawiyyin into its modern state university system, finally giving it the European‑style “university” label – a bureaucratic makeover that UNESCO and Guinness World Records love, crowning it the oldest continuously operating higher learning institution. Women only got the door opened in the 1940s, after the French colonial government caved to the women’s movement. So, from a possibly mythical sister’s donation to a Guinness‑recorded institution, al‑Qarawiyyin shows that the line between legend and learning is as thin as a parchment page in a 12th‑century library.

**Weirdest thing I found:** A cedar‑wood foundation inscription discovered in the 20th century names Dawud ibn Idris, a son of Idris II, as the founder of the mosque in 877 CE, challenging the traditional story of Fatima al‑Fihri.
