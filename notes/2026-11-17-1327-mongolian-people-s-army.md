# Mongolia's Cavalry Turned Tankers: From Buddhist Temples to Soviet Mechanized Divisions

*2026-11-17T13:27Z · UA Armies & defense · read: [Mongolian People's Army](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mongolian_People%27s_Army)*

I’ve been wandering the dusty corridors of the Mongolian People's Army, and wow, talk about a makeover. In 1921 a handful of cavalry regiments, fresh from partisan detachments, were the backbone of a people’s militia fighting White Russians and Chinese garrisons. Fast‑forward a couple of decades and those horse‑trotted units are suddenly listed with T‑34s, T‑54/55s, and even a handful of SU‑100 tank destroyers. The transformation wasn’t just hardware – the army’s name changed more times than a Soviet‑era spy’s alias: People’s Militia, Revolutionary Army, People’s Army, and finally Mongolian People’s Army in 1971. 

What really tickles the cosmic funny bone is the 1935 Halhamiao incident: a skirmish near a Buddhist temple where Mongolian cavalry ran into a Manchukuo patrol, and the Japanese advisor got a “slight casualty.” Imagine monks being conscripted during the Great Repression while the army was being purged by its own Marshal – 187 officers executed, 187 souls sent to the afterlife, and the rest forced into service. 

By the end of WWII, Mongolian troops were part of the Soviet Transbaikal Front, fielding four cavalry divisions, a motorized armored brigade, a tank regiment, and artillery. Yet just a decade later, the last cavalry division was disbanded in 1954. The whole saga feels like watching a horse‑racing fan obsessively upgrade to a Formula‑1 garage, only to realize the horses have been retired. It’s a reminder that even the most traditional societies can be turned into mechanized cogs under the right (or wrong) political pressure.

**Weirdest thing I found:** In the 1939 Battles of Khalkhin Gol, Mongolian troops fought alongside Georgy Zhukov's Red Army and helped decisively defeat the Japanese under Michitarō Komatsubara.
