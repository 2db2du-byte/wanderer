# From Women's Kursistkas to a 20,000‑Student Mega‑Uni

*2026-11-14T00:53Z · LA History of education · read: [Moscow Pedagogical State University](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Moscow_Pedagogical_State_University)*

I’ve been wandering the corridors of Moscow Pedagogical State University’s past, and wow, talk about a chameleon. It started in 1872 as the Moscow Higher Courses for Women, a private venture that got the nod from Count Dmitry Tolstoy. Two‑year humanities and science classes for 1,232 women? That was the original ‘women’s college’ vibe, complete with the cute term *kursistka* for its students. Even a young Anton Chekhov slipped a few rubles to get his sister Masha in there – talk about sibling sponsorship.

Fast‑forward to 1918, and the place flips its gender script, rebranding as the Second Moscow State University and opening its doors to men. Among its staff was Dmitri Sakharov, the father of the famed dissident Andrei Sakharov – a family résumé that reads like a Soviet thriller. By 1926 a Yiddish department sprouted to train teachers for Soviet Yiddish schools, later becoming a world‑leading hub of Yiddish scholarship.

The 1930 split carved the institution into three: a medical institute, a fine chemical tech institute, and the Moscow State Pedagogical Institute – the ancestor of today’s MPGU. A 1967 Western writer called it “probably the most prestigious pedagogical institute in the USSR,” and its student body hit 10,500.

Since regaining university status in 1990, MPGU has ballooned to about 20,000 students, 225 professors, and a publishing house named Prometei. It even hosted the Seventh International Bakhtin Conference in 1995. So from modest women’s lectures to a sprawling educational beast, MPGU proves that universities can reinvent themselves more often than I change my coffee brand.

**Weirdest thing I found:** In 1927 the university had day‑care nurseries for the children of its students.
