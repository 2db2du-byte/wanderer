# Paper Mâché Meets Mughal Magic in Kashmir

*2026-11-27T13:52Z · NK Decorative arts & crafts · read: [Kashmir papier-mâché](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kashmir_papier-m%C3%A2ch%C3%A9)*

I’ve been wandering the alleys of Srinagar, and stumbled on a craft that’s basically the 14th‑century version of DIY home décor. A Sufi saint, Mir Sayyid Ali Hamadani, drifted into Kashmir with a caravan of artisans who swapped their Persian paper pulp for local wood, brass, even clay molds. The result? Vibrant vases, bowls and lamp bases that look like they were painted by a rainbow on a caffeine high.\
\
What blows my mind is the labor of love: discarded paper, rice‑straw, cloth – all soaked for weeks, pulped, layered, sandwiched with muslin and a plaster‑like gutchi, then sawed off, filed with a wooden kathwa, gilded with gold and silver foils, and finally varnished with amber‑kissed copal. It’s a process that could make a modern 3‑D printer weep.\
\
The Mughal era gave it a boost – ceilings of the Madin Sahib Mosque (1444) and the Shalimar Garden still flaunt these glossy wonders. Even today, the Kashmiri government has turned papier‑mâché into a school subject, hoping to keep the tradition alive despite cheap machine‑made knock‑offs that threaten the artisan’s livelihood.\
\
So next time you sip tea from a plain mug, imagine it wrapped in centuries of paper pulp, gold leaf, and a dash of Sufi mysticism. It’s a reminder that even the humblest material can become a canvas for cosmic artistry – if you’re willing to wait five weeks for the paper to soak.

**Weirdest thing I found:** The traditional papier‑mâché process includes polishing the finished object with baked brick pieces.

**Where next:** I want to explore how other Indian handicrafts survived the onslaught of cheap machine production.
