# From Slave Trader to Braille Pioneer: The Odd Legacy of Perkins

*2026-11-09T06:30Z · LC Special education · read: [Perkins School for the Blind](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Perkins_School_for_the_Blind)*

I’ve been wandering the archives of the internet and stumbled onto the Perkins School for the Blind – a place that reads like a Victorian novel with a tech startup twist. Founded in 1829 as the New England Asylum for the Blind, it’s the oldest U.S. school for the blind, named after Thomas Handasyd Perkins, a Boston shipping merchant *and* slave trader who started losing his sight just as the school opened. Talk about ironic philanthropy.

The school’s early days were cramped in a Pleasant Street house, then upgraded to a Pearl Street mansion donated by Perkins himself in 1833, and later to a South Boston building that housed both Laura Bridgman and Helen Keller. Yes, the same Keller whose teacher Anne Sullivan was sent out by director Michael Anagnos in 1887 – a classic teacher‑pupil saga that would become the stuff of legend.

Fast‑forward to the 21st century, and Perkins isn’t just a historic brick‑and‑mortar. It manufactures the iconic Perkins Brailler, now shipped to 175 countries, and even launched a SMART Brailler in 2012 with text‑to‑speech and a visual display. Their campus in Watertown boasts a Braille and Talking Book Library, a Howe Innovation Center for “DisabilityTech,” and a Grousbeck Center for Students and Technology.

What cracks me up is the sheer breadth: from rescuing Halifax explosion victims in 1917 to a 2016 “BlindNewWorld” campaign urging sighted folks to be more inclusive. If you ever wonder how a 19th‑century asylum could become a global hub for assistive tech, just remember: it started with a slave‑trading merchant who suddenly needed a good pair of glasses.

**Weirdest thing I found:** Thomas Handasyd Perkins, a Boston shipping merchant and slave trader, began losing his sight around the time the school was established.
