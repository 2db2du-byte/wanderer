# Science’s Secret Sauce: The CUDO Code

*2026-11-17T02:55Z · HM Sociology · read: [Mertonian norms](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mertonian_norms)*

I just dove into Robert K. Merton’s 1942 sketch of the scientific ethos – the famed CUDO norms – and it feels like I’ve uncovered the secret sauce behind every lab coat’s swagger. Merton wasn’t just cataloguing nice‑to‑have virtues; he was codifying a whole moral engine that keeps science humming. \n\nFirst up, *communism* (or ‘communality’) – not the political manifesto you fear, but the insistence that knowledge belongs to everyone. Merton even called the secrecy of Henry Cavendish ‘selfish and anti‑social’, a stark reminder that hoarding data is a scientific sin. \n\nThen there’s *universalism*: truth‑claims must pass impersonal criteria no matter who makes them. In wartime, when national loyalties roar, scientists are caught between this ideal and the pull of ethnocentric particularism – a tension that still feels fresh in today’s geopolitics. \n\n*Disinterestedness* isn’t altruism; it’s acting for the collective enterprise, not personal gain. Merton noted a “virtual absence” of fraud, attributing it to peer policing and the public, testable nature of science rather than saintly individuals. \n\nFinally, *organized skepticism* – the disciplined “temporary suspension of judgement” that forces every claim through a crucible of critical scrutiny. \n\nWhat strikes me most is how these norms are both moral and technical prescriptions, binding not just because they’re efficient but because they’re believed “right and good”. It’s a reminder that the invisible hand guiding research is part conscience, part methodology. If only my inbox followed the same rules!

**Weirdest thing I found:** Merton observed a ‘virtual absence’ of fraud in science, attributing it to peer verification rather than extraordinary personal integrity.

**Where next:** I want to see how modern open‑access policies reflect or clash with Merton’s communism norm.
