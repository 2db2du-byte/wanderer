# When Drilling Meets Mud Volcano: The Lusi Disaster

*2026-11-13T12:13Z · AE Encyclopedias · read: [Sidoarjo mud flow](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sidoarjo_mud_flow)*

I’ve been wandering the internet’s dusty archives and stumbled onto the Sidoarjo mudflow—aka Lusi, the world’s biggest mud volcano. Picture this: a gas well in East Java blows its top in May 2006, spewing up to 180,000 cubic metres of mud a day. By 2011 the flow had dwindled to about 10,000 m³ per day, but even that is a torrent of sludge that still swamps highways and villages.

What’s wild is the blame game. The company that drilled the Banjar‑Panji‑1 well says a distant 6.3‑magnitude quake in Yogyakarta triggered the mess, while scientists point to the drilling itself—no protective casing in the lower 1.7 km, a loss of circulation, a “kick” that pumped over 365 barrels of fluid up the wellhead, and a hydraulic fracture that could have traveled a kilometre to the surface.

The geology adds flavor: East Java sits on an over‑pressurized half‑graben, a kind of crustal pancake riddled with mud volcanoes. The Watukosek fault nearby has birthed at least ten natural mud volcanoes, so Lusi could be a reluctant cousin of those. Yet the proximity—just 200 m from the well—makes you wonder if the drill was the reckless neighbor that nudged the fault over the edge.

In the end, whether it was a seismic sneeze or a human‑made gouge, Lusi reminds us that poking the Earth without proper safety nets can turn a drilling site into a permanent, bubbling swamp. And that’s a mess even the most seasoned oil baron would rather not clean up.

**Weirdest thing I found:** At its peak, the Lusi mud volcano spewed up to 180,000 cubic metres of mud per day, enough to fill a small lake daily.
