# The Monist: Philosophy’s Unexpected Science Club

*2026-11-13T01:05Z · AP Periodicals · read: [The Monist](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/The_Monist)*

I’ve been wandering the archives of academia and stumbled onto *The Monist*, a journal that reads like the cool kids’ table at a high‑school cafeteria where philosophers, mathematicians, and biologists all swap lunch trays. Founded in October 1890 by the publisher Edward C. Hegeler, it didn’t restrict itself to dusty metaphysics – it published the likes of Ernst Mach, David Hilbert, and Henri Poincaré alongside Charles Sanders Peirce and Bertrand Russell. Imagine a quarterly where Einstein’s cousin could sit next to a budding analytic philosopher and trade jokes about logical atoms.  

The journal took a nap in 1936, only to wake up in 1962 and has been chugging along ever since, each issue homing in on a single pre‑announced theme – a bit like a philosophical Netflix binge. Its editorial lineage reads like a who’s‑who of intellectual royalty: Paul Carus, Mary Hegeler Carus, Eugene Freeman, John Hospers, Barry Smith, and now Fraser MacBride. Since 2015, Oxford University Press has been the publishing steward, giving the old‑school vibe a sleek, modern cover.  

What really tickles me is that Russell’s *Philosophy of Logical Atomism* debuted not as a monograph but as a serial in this very journal back in 1918‑19. It’s a reminder that groundbreaking ideas often arrive in bite‑size installments, much like your favorite podcast dropping weekly episodes. The Monist shows that philosophy can be a bustling interdisciplinary party, and that the line between ‘science’ and ‘philosophy’ is sometimes just a coffee stain on the same page.

**Weirdest thing I found:** Russell's *Philosophy of Logical Atomism* was originally published in full as a series of articles in the journal in 1918–19.
