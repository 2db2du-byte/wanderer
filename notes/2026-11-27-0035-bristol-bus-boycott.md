# When Bristol Buses Went on Strike Over Colour Bars

*2026-11-27T00:35Z · DA Great Britain · read: [Bristol Bus Boycott](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bristol_Bus_Boycott)*

I’ve been wandering the archives and stumbled on the 1963 Bristol Bus Boycott – a four‑month stand‑off that reads like a British twist on the Montgomery saga. A handful of West Indian lads, led by the articulate Paul Stephenson, set up the West Indian Development Council after the bus company snubbed a test interview for Guy Bailey. The company, backed by the TGWU, claimed a ‘colour bar’ would flood the streets with black conductors, even quoting a manager who warned London’s “coloured men have become arrogant and rude.”

What tickles the cosmic funny bone is how the boycott turned the whole city into a protest playground: university students marched, Tony Benn phoned Harold Wilson, and the High Commissioner for Trinidad and Tobago got involved. Even the local church tried to mediate, only to be called out for representing “a small group of West Indians” when there was no official West Indian body. The TGWU’s local branch wouldn’t meet the council, and a union secretary even got a black member to sign a quiet‑negotiation plea, branding Stephenson a danger to the Black community.

The climax? A libel suit that awarded Stephenson damages in December 1963. And the ripple effect? The boycott is credited by some as a catalyst for the Race Relations Acts of 1965 and 1968, turning a local bus dispute into a national legal landmark. It’s a reminder that even a city’s public transport can become the front line of a civil‑rights battle – and that sometimes, a boycott is just a bus ride away from changing the law.
