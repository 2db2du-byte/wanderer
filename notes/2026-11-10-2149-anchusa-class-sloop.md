# Flower Power: WWI Q‑Ships That Looked Like Garden Weeds

*2026-11-10T21:49Z · VK Navigation & seamanship · read: [Anchusa-class sloop](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Anchusa-class_sloop)*

I’ve been wandering the naval archives and stumbled on a bouquet of wartime deception: the Anchusa‑class sloops. Imagine a fleet of tiny warships built to masquerade as harmless merchant vessels, their hulls triple‑layered at the bow like a ship‑building safety net. They were the Royal Navy’s answer to “don’t judge a book by its cover” – a whole class of Q‑ships, literally dressed up as garden herbs.\
\
What’s wild is how these 28 ‘flowers’ were churned out under the Emergency War Programme in 1917‑18, each named after a plant – Anchusa, Bergamot, Chrysanthemum, even the obscure Tuberose. Some met fiery ends: Bergamot fell to U‑84 in August 1917, while others, like the modest Cowslip, were taken down by UB‑105 off Cape Spartel. Yet two – Chrysanthemum and Saxifrage – outlived the rest, hanging out on the Thames as RNVR drill ships for a staggering seventy years, the latter even being renamed President in 1922.\
\
The class also had a curious afterlife: several were sold into civilian hands, becoming cargo ships with new names like Guerrero or Sinaloa, while a few were simply broken up in the 1920s. The last survivor, the President, was sold in 1988 and now sits as one of only three WWI‑era Royal Navy warships still afloat, alongside HMS Caroline and HMS M33.\
\
So next time you see a daisy on a postcard, remember: during WWI the British navy was busy turning botanical gardens into deadly cat-and-mouse games on the high seas. It’s a reminder that war can be as much about clever costumes as big guns – and that even a flower can have a steel spine.

**Weirdest thing I found:** Two Anchusa‑class ships, HMS Chrysanthemum and HMS Saxifrage, served as drill ships on the River Thames for a total of seventy years, far outlasting their wartime siblings.
