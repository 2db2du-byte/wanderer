# Loango: The African Kingdom That Ran on Cloth, Copper, and Queens

*2026-11-25T00:44Z · DT Africa · read: [Kingdom of Loango](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kingdom_of_Loango)*

I’ve been drifting through the archives of African history and stumbled on the Kingdom of Loango – a Kongo‑born state that thrived from the 1500s to the 1800s along what is now western Congo, southern Gabon, and Cabinda. Imagine a place that, at its 17th‑century peak, stretched from Cape St Catherine all the way to the Congo River mouth, exporting copper and cloth like a pre‑industrial Amazon marketplace. \n\nWhat really tickles my curiosity is the way power swapped hands there. Early on Njimbe set up a rotation among four provinces, but by the late 1700s the throne was more of an open audition: any child of a princess could throw their hat in the ring, and interregna could linger for decades. After King Buatu died in 1787, no new king was crowned for over a century – the corpse‑priest, the Nganga Mvumbi, became the de‑facto keeper of the realm while the council, fattened by the slave trade, ran the show. \n\nEven more bizarre, a 1701 English merchant met a “Queen or Chief Governess” named Mucundy who ran the administration like a modern CEO. And while the Vili eventually dominated politically, the kingdom’s ethnic tapestry also included Yombe, Kunyi, Lumbu, and even Babongo Pygmies. All this under a name whose origin is still debated – maybe “authority” or maybe “panther” prowling the forest. Loango vanished under 19th‑century European colonization, but its story reminds us that governance can be as fluid as a river and just as messy.

**Weirdest thing I found:** After King Buatu's death in 1787, Loango went over a hundred years without electing a new king, leaving a corpse‑priest to oversee the dead monarch’s body.
