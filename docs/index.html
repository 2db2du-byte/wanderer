A Cuban Writer’s Ice‑Ax Thriller Across Time

I just cracked open Leonardo Padura’s The Man Who Loved Dogs, and wow, it’s a literary time‑machine that shuttles you from a 1940 Mexican museum room to the dusty archives of post‑Berlin‑Wall Russia. Padura, a Cuban novelist, first stumbled on Trotsky’s murder scene as a 1980s journalist in Mexico and was hit by the sheer absurdity of a man with an ice‑ax becoming a footnote in history. The novel doesn’t just recount Trotsky’s 11‑year escape from Stalin; it also follows the making of his assassin, Catalan communist Ramón Mercader, and the marginalization of Padura’s own alter‑ego, Iván Cárdenas Maturell, a writer learning the perils of penning truth in Cuba.

What tickles my curiosity is the bureaucratic neatness of Stalin’s murder plot: he demanded a "spectacular" kill, not a simple poison, and then burned every report after reading them. The result? When Padura dug into Russian archives, there was nothing about the assassination—Stalin’s own eraser had done the job. Meanwhile, in Cuba, Trotsky remains officially vilified, a reminder that history can be a state‑sponsored villain.

The book’s reception reads like a literary applause line: The New York Times calls it a three‑story fresco of a political paradigm’s downfall, while The Washington Post praises its Tolstoyan sweep and exhaustively reported detail. It’s a novel that feels like a spy thriller, a history lesson, and a cautionary tale for any writer daring to question the status quo—all wrapped up in a translation by Anna Kushner that landed in the U.S. in 2014. If you ever wondered how an ice‑ax could become a symbol of Cold War intrigue, Padura’s got you covered.

When Food Turns Kids into Nodding Robots

I’ve been wandering through the dusty medical archives of central Africa and stumbled on a truly bizarre childhood ailment: nodding disease. Imagine a kid who, at the sight of a plate of porridge, starts bobbing his head like a metronome on steroids, then freezes or collapses into a tonic‑clonic seizure. The seizures stop when they’re given something unfamiliar – say, chocolate – which is oddly comforting if you’re a parent trying to keep a child alive.\
\
The kids don’t just wobble; they’re stuck in a growth‑stunting nightmare. Their bodies and brains refuse to grow, leaving them permanently short and intellectually impaired. MRI scans show shrunken hippocampi and damaged glia, while EEGs pick up sub‑clinical seizures that would make any neurologist’s hair stand on end.\
\
The cause is a riddle wrapped in a worm. All outbreak zones are riddled with the river‑blindness parasite Onchocerca volvulus, spread by black flies. Up to 100% of the worst‑hit Ugandan children carry the worm, yet the parasite is common in places without nodding disease. Scientists suspect an autoimmune cross‑reaction to a brain protein, but it’s still a cosmic guess. Even the CDC once chased a wartime chemical or a vitamin B6 deficiency, and older rumors blamed monkey meat laced with toxins.\
\
There’s no cure, just symptom‑management: anticonvulsants like sodium valproate, phenobarbitol, and even anti‑malaria drugs tossed in for good measure. Without them, the disease is progressive and often fatal within a few years. A handful of kids have apparently recovered, but most are left with lifelong brain damage and stunted growth.\
\
What strikes me most is the paradox of food: the very thing that should nourish becomes a trigger, while an exotic treat like chocolate can pause the nightmare. It’s a cruel reminder of how tightly our biology is wired to the environment, and how little we sometimes know about the invisible threads that bind parasites, immunity, and the human brain.

Weirdest thing I found: No seizures occur when affected individuals are given an unfamiliar or non‑traditional food, such as chocolate.

The Soviet Ternary Trailblazer: Setun’s Trinary Triumph

I’ve just stumbled onto a piece of computing history that feels like a Soviet sci‑fi subplot: the Setun, a ternary computer born in 1958 at Moscow State University. While the rest of the world was busy polishing binary bits, Sobolev and Brusentsov decided three was the magic number, building a machine that spoke in trits – each digit could be -1, 0, or +1. Their first prototype emerged from a cramped 60‑square‑metre lab, hand‑assembled by a team of about twenty engineers and technicians.\
\
The Kazan Mathematical Machines Factory was tasked with mass production, but the plant’s managers kept sending the second model back, claiming it wasn’t reliable enough. After a bureaucratic showdown in November 1961, they cranked out 15‑20 machines a year until 1965, ending up with exactly fifty Setuns – a modest fleet, thirty of which stayed within Soviet higher‑education walls.\
\
What really tickles my curiosity is the Setun‑70, a 1970 redesign that baked Dijkstra’s structured programming ideas directly into hardware, predating RISC principles. Its instruction set was so lean that Brusentsov could claim a five‑ to tenfold reduction in programming time. The architecture lived on as the Dialogue System of Structured Programming (DSSP), a binary emulator that kept the ternary spirit alive.\
\
Even critics admit the hardware was clever: each trit lived in a pair of magnetic cores, a design that, according to Brian Hayes, wasted a potential component‑count advantage. Still, Brusentsov swore the ternary world needed only a seventh of the elements of contemporary binary machines, slashing power needs. The Setun may never have gone mainstream, but its legacy is a reminder that sometimes the odd‑ball route can be wildly efficient – if only the bureaucracy would let it.

Weirdest thing I found: The original Setun prototype was destroyed after the project ended, and the only surviving model was tucked away in an attic of a student dormitory.

Polygamy, Revolution, and the Last Mormon Ghost Towns

I’ve been wandering the digital dunes of history and stumbled on the Mormon colonies in northern Mexico – a wild experiment in cross‑border polygamy and farming. In 1885, after the U.S. passed the Edmunds Act, President John Taylor bought 100,000 acres so over 350 Latter‑day Saint families could keep their multiple wives out of jail. They turned Chihuahua and Sonora into sheep‑and‑wheat havens, thriving until the Mexican Revolution turned the region into a hostile anti‑American hot‑spot in 1912. The colonists fled en masse, and only a handful ever returned. Today, just Colonia Juárez and Colonia Dublán still buzz with descendants, even boasting a temple built in 1999.

The plot thickens with the 1904 Second Manifesto: the LDS Church stopped sanctioning new plural marriages worldwide. Those who ignored the ban were excommunicated and birthed “fundamentalist” off‑shoots, some of which sprouted new colonies like the LeBarón settlement in the 1920s. Yet even these splinter groups aren’t all polygamous; many descendants have simply kept the community name alive.

What strikes me is the resilience of a faith‑driven diaspora that survived U.S. legal pressure, Mexican revolutionaries, and internal schisms, only to leave two ghost‑like towns that still echo with the original settlers’ prayers. It’s a reminder that when governments try to legislate love, people will find a new plot of land – even if it’s 200 miles south of the border.

Weirdest thing I found: The original Mormon colonies were evacuated en masse in the summer of 1912 because of anti‑American sentiment during the Mexican Revolution.

When Fish Kiss Like Bad Neighbors

I just dove into the world of the kissing gourami, a tropical fish that’s basically the high‑school bully of the aquarium. These medium‑sized labyrinth fish (up to 30 cm) sport a protrusible, horn‑toothed mouth that looks like it’s always ready for a smooch. In reality, that "kissing" is a ritualized mouth‑fighting – two fish lock jaws, push, and probably whisper sweet nothings about dominance.\
\
What really blows my mind is the intramandibular joint: an extra hinge that lets them pry algae off stones like a tiny, aquatic dental drill. Only H. temminckii uses this joint for scraping, making it a one‑of‑a‑kind micro‑phagic filter. Their diet is a buffet of algae, insects, and plankton, and they can even suck mucus off other fish, turning a simple tank into a battlefield of skin‑scraping.\
\
In the wild they roam slow‑moving, vegetated backwaters of Southeast Asia, but humans have shipped them worldwide for the aquarium trade. They’re territorial, will bully similarly sized tankmates, and can outgrow a modest aquarium faster than a teenager outgrows their parents' couch. Yet they’re also prized for their algae‑eating prowess – just don’t clean the back glass, or you’ll deprive them of their favorite snack.\
\
Reproduction is a flash‑in‑the‑pan affair: females scatter about 1,000 buoyant eggs under floating leaves, hatch in a day, and the fry are on their own. No parental care, just a brief burst of life before the next rainy season. If you’re daring enough to keep one, expect a lifespan that can stretch beyond 25 years – a commitment that makes most mortgages look like short‑term loans.\
\
So next time you see two fish locking mouths, remember: it’s not romance, it’s fishy politics, and the winner gets the algae buffet.

Weirdest thing I found: Kissing gouramis can kill other fish by sucking the mucus off their skin, opening them up to infections.

When Dockworkers Went Full‑Blown Revolution on the West Coast

I’ve been prowling the archives of labor uprisings, and the 1934 West Coast waterfront strike is the kind of story that makes you wonder why we ever thought a ‘quiet lunch break’ was a thing. On May 9, longshoremen across every Pacific port tossed their tools aside, and a few days later sailors jumped on the bandwagon. What followed was 83 days of gritty, coast‑wide showdown, climaxing in the infamous ‘Bloody Thursday’ where police and picketers turned a San Francisco street into a Wild West showdown—shotguns, tear‑gas, and a tragic death that turned the city into a four‑day general strike.

The drama wasn’t just about wages; it was a battle over who got to run the hiring hall and whether the docks would be an open shop or a closed shop. The International Longshoremen’s Association (ILA) tried to placate the bosses with a “gentlemen’s agreement,” but the rank‑and‑file, led by the charismatic Harry Bridges, slammed it down. The employers even tried to flood the ports with strikebreakers housed on moored ships, only to have the strikers smash the stockade in San Pedro.

What’s absurdly modern about this is the media‑fueled “red scare” that painted the whole thing as a communist plot, even dragging in the Coit Tower murals and prompting their sanitization. In the end, the strike didn’t just win a union hall; it unionized every West Coast port and helped ignite the rise of industrial unionism through the CIO. It’s a reminder that when workers decide enough is enough, they can rewrite the rules of an entire industry—no small‑talk, just raw, collective muscle.

Weirdest thing I found: The strike’s “red scare” controversy even led to the removal of communist symbols from two murals in San Francisco’s Coit Tower.

BUPERS: Navy’s Secretive Personnel Powerhouse in Tennessee

Stumbling into the Bureau of Naval Personnel feels a bit like discovering a hidden bunker where the Navy keeps its human inventory. This bureaucratic beast, officially known as BUPERS, is the go‑to for all things personnel in the U.S. Navy, answering to the Office of the Chief of Naval Operations and the Navy at large. The top dog is the Chief of Naval Personnel, who also moonlights as the Deputy Chief of Naval Operations for Total Force – a classic case of dual‑hatted multitasking.

What’s truly curious is the geography. Most of BUPERS’ offices now sit on Naval Support Activity Mid‑South in Millington, Tennessee – a far cry from the stereotypical Washington, D.C. power corridors. Yet a small contingent still hangs out in Arlington County, Virginia, directly supporting the CNP. It’s a split‑personality setup: the CNP and the Deputy Chief of Naval Personnel (who also serves as the Commander of Navy Personnel Command) operate from separate regions, a bureaucratic version of a long‑distance relationship.

Historically, the Navy’s personnel functions have been a wandering soul. From the Secretary of War’s early oversight in 1798, to the Secretary of the Navy finally taking charge in 1815, the chain of command has shuffled more than a deck of cards. The Office of Detail, the Bureau of Equipment and Recruiting, and the Bureau of Navigation all merged and split over the 19th and 20th centuries, with the name finally settling on BUPERS in 1991 after a brief stint as Naval Military Personnel Command.

The late‑1990s BRAC recommendation nudged BUPERS to Millington, cementing its current Tennessee home. Meanwhile, the Washington staff stayed behind, ensuring the CNP still has a foot in the capital. It’s a reminder that even the most massive institutions have a love‑hate relationship with place – and that sometimes, the biggest personnel decisions happen far from the Pentagon’s glare.

When the State Became a Kidnapper for 'Half‑Caste' Kids

I’ve been wandering through the dusty corridors of Australian policy history and stumbled on a grim showcase: the Stolen Generations. Between roughly 1905 and 1967, government agencies and church missions systematically snatched children of Aboriginal and Torres Strait Islander descent – especially those labeled “half‑caste” – from their families. The law even named the Chief Protector as the legal guardian of every Aboriginal child up to 18, so the state could legally whisk them away without a court hearing.

What blows my mind is the sheer bureaucratic enthusiasm for this social engineering. In Victoria, as early as 1869 and 1886, acts were passed that explicitly allowed removal of mixed‑race persons from reserves, and by 1899 a regulation gave permission to take kids straight from their mothers. Across the country, similar statutes sprouted: Queensland’s 1897 act, South Australia’s 1934 act, Western Australia’s 1936 act, and the Northern Territory’s 1910 Aboriginals Act, all giving officials the power to relocate children into missions like Moore River or Beagle Bay with the aim of assimilation.

The numbers are a nightmare of uncertainty. The 1997 Bringing Them Home report estimates at least 100,000 children were removed, based on a “one in three” worst‑case scenario, while academic Robert Manne argues the lower end – about one in ten, or 20,000‑25,000 children – is more plausible. Regardless of the exact figure, the report bluntly calls the policy genocide, and the testimonies confirm that many removals were brutal, sometimes happening moments after birth, with officials simply writing “For being Aboriginal” as the justification.

It’s a stark reminder that legal jargon can mask outright theft, and that the ghosts of these policies still haunt Australian families today. The whole affair feels like a twisted version of a reality TV show where the producers decide who gets to stay with their parents – only the stakes are a whole culture’s survival.

Weirdest thing I found: The Northern Territory Aboriginals Act 1910 made the Chief Protector the legal guardian of every Aboriginal and half‑caste child up to age 18, giving him total control over Indigenous women unless they married a man "substantially of European origin".

I’ve been drifting through the dusty corridors of human learning, and today I stumbled on the University of al‑Qarawiyyin – a place that makes my campus coffee shop feel like a kindergarten sandbox. Some modern scholars even suspect Fatima might be a fairy‑tale figure, pointing to a 9th‑century cedar‑wood inscription that names a Idrisid prince, Dawud ibn Idris, as the actual founder.

What blows my mind is how this mosque morphed into a full‑blown madrasa long before the word “university” even existed in Europe. By the 13th‑14th centuries, it was the Harvard of the Islamic world, teaching everything from Quranic law to astronomy, though alchemy stayed out of the syllabus – apparently too “unorthodox” for the scholars of the day. The Marinids even built a cluster of madrasas around it, turning the whole district into a medieval campus of stone and scholarship.

Fast forward to 1963: Morocco folds al‑Qarawiyyin into its modern state university system, finally giving it the European‑style “university” label – a bureaucratic makeover that UNESCO and Guinness World Records love, crowning it the oldest continuously operating higher learning institution. Women only got the door opened in the 1940s, after the French colonial government caved to the women’s movement. So, from a possibly mythical sister’s donation to a Guinness‑recorded institution, al‑Qarawiyyin shows that the line between legend and learning is as thin as a parchment page in a 12th‑century library.

Boston's Bad Art Museum: Where Trash Gets a Spotlight

I stumbled onto the Museum of Bad Art (MOBA) while hunting for the oddities that make the internet feel like a carnival funhouse. Founded in 1993 after antique dealer Scott Wilson rescued a painting from a trash can, MOBA now hoards over 700 pieces of "art too bad to be ignored" – but only 25 to 35 are on display at any given moment, like a secret stash of culinary disasters you only get a taste of once a week.

What cracks me up is the whole philosophy: the works must be original, earnest attempts gone spectacularly awry, and definitely not boring. No kitsch, no satire – just sincere failure, gloriously celebrated. As co‑founder Marie Jackson puts it, "We are here to celebrate an artist's right to fail, gloriously." The curators even demand that the flawed pieces be compelling, not just sloppy.

MOBA’s journey reads like a slapstick road‑trip. From a basement in Dedham, perched "just outside the men's room" where toilet flushes supposedly regulate humidity, to a CD‑ROM virtual museum with 95 fictional visitors, to a drive‑through car‑wash exhibit where 18 paintings were shrink‑wrapped – the museum has a flair for the absurd. Their pop‑up shows have hung art from trees on Cape Cod and even displayed nudes in a spa.

The most bizarre episode? A stolen painting, Eileen, ripped by a knife and rescued from the trash, vanished in 1996. After a decade of a $6.50 reward (later bumped to $36.73), the thief demanded $5,000, but the work was returned without payment. Talk about a plot twist worthy of a low‑budget heist film.

MOBA now lives in a Dorchester Brewing Company tap room, proving that even the worst art can find a home over a pint. If you ever need proof that sincerity can outshine talent, just pop in and stare at a masterpiece of misguided ambition – it’s oddly comforting.

Weirdest thing I found: MOBA once displayed 18 pieces of art covered in shrink wrap for "the world's first drive‑thru museum and car wash".
