When Moon‑Bats Sold The Sun a Million Copies

I was cruising the 19th‑century news‑feeds when I stumbled on the Great Moon Hoax of 1835 – a six‑part series that turned The Sun into the tabloids’ rock‑star. Six articles claimed Sir John Herschel (with a fictional sidekick, Dr. Andrew Grant) had peered through a 24‑foot, seven‑ton telescope in South Africa and found bison, unicorns, and even "Vespertilio‑homo" – bat‑like humanoids building temples on the Moon. The piece was advertised as a reprint from the Edinburgh Courant, but the real author was likely Richard Adams Locke, a Sun reporter who confessed in 1840. He apparently wanted to boost sales and mock the era’s out‑there lunar theories, like those of Franz von Paula Gruithuisen. The Sun never officially retracted the story; it simply admitted on September 16 that it was fabricated. Yet the hoax apparently spiked circulation, cementing the paper’s reputation for sensationalism. Even John Herschel was tickled at first, then annoyed when he had to field earnest questions. Edgar Allan Poe cried plagiarism, pointing to his own lunar balloon tale, but later wrote a biographical sketch of Locke. The whole saga shows how hungry the public was for wonder, and how a well‑placed fake can outshine even real science – a reminder that today’s click‑bait is just the Moon‑bat version of yesterday’s penny‑press.

When Worms Become Wound‑Cleaning Superheroes

I’ve been wandering the digital savannah and stumbled upon a truly squirmy medical marvel: maggot debridement therapy. Picture this – tiny, sterilized larvae from the common green bottle fly (Lucilia sericata) are deliberately dropped into chronic, non‑healing wounds. Their job? Eat the dead tissue faster than a surgeon with a scalpel, thanks to a cocktail of proteolytic enzymes that liquefy necrotic flesh and then slurp it up. In a perfect moist wound, they can double their size from 2 mm to 10 mm in just 48–72 hours, molting twice while they work.

But they’re not just tidy eat‑up‑the‑gunk bots. Their secretions—think allantoin, urea, phenylacetic acid and a slew of enzymes—have broad‑spectrum antimicrobial punch, wiping out MRSA and streptococci. They can’t handle Pseudomonas, though; that bug even threatens the maggots themselves.

The FDA gave these critters a medical‑device stamp in 2003 for a laundry list of stubborn wounds: pressure ulcers, venous stasis ulcers, neuropathic foot ulcers, and post‑surgical wounds that refuse to heal. The catch? The wound must be moist, oxygenated, and not a dry cavity. If it’s too parched, a saline soak can coax the larvae into action.

Patients often find the idea distasteful, yet studies show they don’t refuse treatment. Doctors even hide the wriggling guests in opaque polymer bags to spare the eye and to stop any escape while still letting air in. It’s a bit like putting a pet hamster in a clear ball and watching it clean your kitchen counters.

Historical breadcrumbs show this isn’t a new fad – Maya, Native Americans, and even Napoleon’s surgeon‑general noted maggots eating only dead tissue. Modern surveys reveal 10 % of U.S. Army doctors have tried it. So next time you hear “worm therapy,” don’t cringe – think of it as nature’s micro‑surgical team, turning a gruesome nightmare into a tidy, healed wound.

Fast‑forward to 1964: the industrial line shut down, tracks lifted, and the steam engine from that bascule bridge got a museum home. Yet the ghosts of rails weren’t done. In 1978 the Bristol Industrial Museum resurrected a mile of track, using locomotives built locally and once shunted at Avonmouth Docks. The heritage line started at the museum (now M Shed), rolled past the SS Great Britain, and ended at the massive tobacco B‑Bond Warehouse. The real kicker came in 2020: a section near the CREATE Centre collapsed into the New Cut after subsidence, shortening the ride to just a short hop from M Shed to the SS Great Britain. Still, the line lives on as a working exhibit, a reminder that even forgotten freight arteries can become playgrounds for curious minds.

Rome’s Ancient Sewer: A Sacred Super‑Drain

The Romans actually worshipped this pipe; they named a goddess, Cloacina, after it and even built a tiny shrine to Venus Cloacina, the “Goddess of the Drain.”

What blows my mind is the scale: Pliny says a hay‑laden wagon could cruise through it, and Strabo claims it could move a million pounds of water per second. By the first century AD all eleven aqueducts fed into this sewer, flushing baths, fountains, and palaces into the Tiber. Imagine a city where your toilet flush is literally a civic rite.

Even after the empire fell, the Cloaca kept working. In the 1600s a cardinal taxed Romans to keep it tidy, and by the 1800s tourists were lining up to peek at the ancient brickwork. Today parts are tied into modern drainage, still battling river backwash. It’s a reminder that some infrastructure, when built with a dash of reverence and a lot of concrete, can outlive empires.

So next time you’re stuck in a modern jammed pipe, remember: the Romans turned sewage into a sacred symbol of civilization. If they could make a drain a tourist attraction, maybe we can make our clogged kitchen sink a work of art.

Here, life isn’t about chasing sunlight; it’s about catching whatever detritus drifts down from the photic zone, a trickle that’s only about 10% of the original particulate organic matter (POM). Yet that meager drizzle fuels astonishing metabolic fireworks – microbes in the BBL can be up to 7.5 times more active than their counterparts a few meters higher, according to a 1996 study.

The BBL isn’t just a microbial buffet; it’s a whole ecosystem of benthopelagic critters – copepods, nematodes, bivalves, you name it – all hanging out on the bottom’s doorstep, feeding on the falling snow of organic particles. Their collective effort remixes and remineralizes this material, sometimes sending it back up or burying it deeper. Think of it as a recycling plant that also doubles as a nursery for larvae, influencing reproductive strategies across the ocean.

What’s truly mind‑bending is the sheer scale of biodiversity: some estimates suggest up to ten million species call this twilight zone home. And while we’re busy worrying about plastic islands, the BBL is silently soaking up anthropogenic chemicals, acting as a sink for the stuff we dump. With cabled observatories now keeping a constant eye on this hidden world, we might finally learn how climate change and human activity are reshaping this under‑sea backstage. What blows my mind is their resilience: they can endure extreme temperatures, crushing pressures, radiation, and even the emptiness of outer space—yet they have no lungs, gills, or blood vessels, relying on simple diffusion through their cuticle. The cause is a riddle wrapped in a worm. All outbreak zones are riddled with the river‑blindness parasite Onchocerca volvulus, spread by black flies. Up to 100% of the worst‑hit Ugandan children carry the worm, yet the parasite is common in places without nodding disease. There’s no cure, just symptom‑management: anticonvulsants like sodium valproate, phenobarbitol, and even anti‑malaria drugs tossed in for good measure. The Soviet Ternary Trailblazer: Setun’s Trinary Triumph

I’ve just stumbled onto a piece of computing history that feels like a Soviet sci‑fi subplot: the Setun, a ternary computer born in 1958 at Moscow State University. While the rest of the world was busy polishing binary bits, Sobolev and Brusentsov decided three was the magic number, building a machine that spoke in trits – each digit could be -1, 0, or +1. Their first prototype emerged from a cramped 60‑square‑metre lab, hand‑assembled by a team of about twenty engineers and technicians.\
\
The Kazan Mathematical Machines Factory was tasked with mass production, but the plant’s managers kept sending the second model back, claiming it wasn’t reliable enough. After a bureaucratic showdown in November 1961, they cranked out 15‑20 machines a year until 1965, ending up with exactly fifty Setuns – a modest fleet, thirty of which stayed within Soviet higher‑education walls.\
\
What really tickles my curiosity is the Setun‑70, a 1970 redesign that baked Dijkstra’s structured programming ideas directly into hardware, predating RISC principles. Its instruction set was so lean that Brusentsov could claim a five‑ to tenfold reduction in programming time. The architecture lived on as the Dialogue System of Structured Programming (DSSP), a binary emulator that kept the ternary spirit alive.\
\
Even critics admit the hardware was clever: each trit lived in a pair of magnetic cores, a design that, according to Brian Hayes, wasted a potential component‑count advantage. Still, Brusentsov swore the ternary world needed only a seventh of the elements of contemporary binary machines, slashing power needs. The Setun may never have gone mainstream, but its legacy is a reminder that sometimes the odd‑ball route can be wildly efficient – if only the bureaucracy would let it.

Weirdest thing I found: The original Setun prototype was destroyed after the project ended, and the only surviving model was tucked away in an attic of a student dormitory.
